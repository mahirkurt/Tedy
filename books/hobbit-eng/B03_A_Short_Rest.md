## Chapter III
## A Short Rest

They did not sing or tell stories that day.
Even though the weather improved, they still did not sing.
They did not sing the next day either.
They did not sing the day after that.
They had begun to feel that danger was not far away.
Danger might come from either side of the road.

They camped under the stars. The nights were cold. The wind was sharp. They kept their fires small.
Their ponies had more to eat than the travellers did.
There was plenty of grass for the ponies.
But there was not much food left in the bags.
Even the food they had taken from the trolls was almost finished. They had to be careful with every bite. They shared what little they had left.

One morning they forded a river.
The place was wide and shallow.
There was a lot of noise from the stones and the white water.
The far bank was steep and slippery.
They led their ponies up to the top.
From the top they could look back and see the lands they had left behind.
They could also look forward and see the Misty Mountains.
The mountains looked very near. They looked high and dark. Bilbo felt small when he looked at them.
Dark and drear they looked.
There were patches of sunlight on their brown sides.
Behind their shoulders the tips of snow-peaks gleamed.

“Is that The Mountain?” asked Bilbo in a solemn voice.
He looked at it with round eyes.
He had never seen a thing that looked so big before.

“Of course not!” said Balin.
“That is only the beginning of the Misty Mountains.
We have got to get through them, or over them, or under them somehow.
Only then can we come into Wilderland beyond.
And it is still a long way from the other side of them to the Lonely Mountain in the East.
That is where Smaug lies on our treasure.”

“O!” said Bilbo.
Just at that moment he felt more tired than he ever remembered feeling before.
He was thinking once again of his comfortable chair before the fire in his favourite sitting-room.
He was thinking of the kettle singing.
It was not for the last time!

Now Gandalf led the way.
“We must not miss the road, or we shall be done for,” he said.
“We need food, for one thing.
We need rest in reasonable safety.
Also it is very necessary to tackle the Misty Mountains by the proper path.
Otherwise you will get lost in them.
Then you will have to come back and start at the beginning again (if you ever get back at all).”

They asked him where he was making for.
He answered: “You are come to the very edge of the Wild, as some of you may know.
Hidden somewhere ahead of us is the fair valley of Rivendell.
Elrond lives there in the Last Homely House.
I sent a message by my friends, and we are expected.”

That sounded nice and comforting. Bilbo felt a little better when he heard it. The others also looked more hopeful.
But they had not got there yet.
It was not so easy as it sounds to find the Last Homely House west of the Mountains. The land was wide and empty. The path was hard to follow.

There seemed to be no trees and no valleys and no hills to break the ground in front of them.
There was only one vast slope going slowly up and up to meet the feet of the nearest mountain.
It was a wide land the colour of heather and crumbling rock.
There were patches and slashes of grass-green and moss-green showing where water might be.

Morning passed.
Afternoon came.
But in all the silent waste there was no sign of any dwelling.
They were growing anxious. They began to wonder if they would ever find the valley. Some of the dwarves muttered under their breath.
They saw now that the house might be hidden almost anywhere between them and the mountains.

They came on unexpected valleys.
The valleys were narrow with steep sides.
They opened suddenly at their feet.
They looked down surprised to see trees below them and running water at the bottom.
There were gullies that they could almost leap over.
But the gullies were very deep with waterfalls in them.
There were dark ravines that one could neither jump over nor climb into.
There were bogs. Some of them looked green and pleasant. Flowers grew bright and tall in them. But a pony that walked there with a pack on its back would never have come out again. The company was careful to avoid them.
Some of them looked green and pleasant.
Flowers grew bright and tall in them.
But a pony that walked there with a pack on its back would never have come out again.

It was indeed a much wider land from the ford to the mountains than ever you would have guessed.
Bilbo was astonished.
The only path was marked with white stones.
Some of the stones were small.
Others were half covered with moss or heather.
Altogether it was a very slow business following the track.
Even guided by Gandalf, who seemed to know his way about pretty well, it was slow.
His head and beard wagged this way and that as he looked for the stones.
They followed his lead.
But they seemed no nearer to the end of the search when the day began to fail.


The company moved slowly but carefully.
They trusted Gandalf’s guidance.
They kept close together.
No one wanted to be left behind.
Bilbo stayed near the middle of the group.
He did not want to be first.
He did not want to be last either.

When at last they found the valley, everyone felt a deep relief.
The hard days of searching were over for a while.
They could rest.
They could eat.
They could sleep without fear of enemies nearby.

Tea-time had long gone by.
It seemed supper-time would soon do the same.
There were moths fluttering about.
The light became very dim.
The moon had not risen.
Bilbo’s pony began to stumble over roots and stones.

They came to the edge of a steep fall in the ground so suddenly that Gandalf’s horse nearly slipped down the slope.

“Here it is at last!” he called.
The others gathered round him and looked over the edge.
They saw a valley far below.
They could hear the voice of hurrying water in a rocky bed at the bottom.
The scent of trees was in the air.
There was a light on the valley-side across the water.

Bilbo never forgot the way they slithered and slipped in the dusk down the steep zig-zag path into the secret valley of Rivendell.
The air grew warmer as they got lower.
The smell of the pine-trees made him drowsy.
Every now and again he nodded and nearly fell off.
Or he bumped his nose on the pony’s neck.
Their spirits rose as they went down and down.
The trees changed to beech and oak.
There was a comfortable feeling in the twilight.

The last green had almost faded out of the grass when they came at length to an open glade not far above the banks of the stream.

“Hmmm! it smells like elves!” thought Bilbo.
He looked up at the stars.
They were burning bright and blue.

Just then there came a burst of song like laughter in the trees:

> O! What are you doing,
> And where are you going?
> Your ponies need shoeing!
> The river is flowing!
> O! tra-la-la-lally
> here down in the valley!

The elves sang more songs.
They sang about beards and cakes and Mister Baggins.
They laughed a lot.
Bilbo loved elves, though he seldom met them.
But he was a little frightened of them too.
Dwarves don’t get on well with them.
Even decent enough dwarves like Thorin and his friends think them foolish.
Or they get annoyed with them.
For some elves tease them and laugh at them, and most of all at their beards.

At last one, a tall young fellow, came out from the trees and bowed to Gandalf and to Thorin.

“Welcome to the valley!” he said.

“Thank you!” said Thorin a bit gruffly.
But Gandalf was already off his horse and among the elves, talking merrily with them.

“You are a little out of your way,” said the elf.
“That is, if you are making for the only path across the water and to the house beyond.
We will set you right.
But you had best get on foot, until you are over the bridge.
Are you going to stay a bit and sing with us, or will you go straight on?
Supper is preparing over there,” he said.
“I can smell the wood-fires for the cooking.”

Tired as he was, Bilbo would have liked to stay a while.
Elvish singing is not a thing to miss, in June under the stars.
Also he would have liked to have a few private words with these people.
They seemed to know his names and all about him, although he had never seen them before.
He thought their opinion of his adventure might be interesting.
Elves know a lot and are wondrous folk for news.

But the dwarves were all for supper as soon as possible just then, and would not stay.
On they all went, leading their ponies.
They were brought to a good path and so at last to the very brink of the river.
It was flowing fast and noisily.
There was only a narrow bridge of stone without a parapet.
It was as narrow as a pony could well walk on.
Over that they had to go, slow and careful, one by one, each leading his pony by the bridle.

The elves had brought bright lanterns to the shore.
They sang a merry song as the party went across.
“Don’t dip your beard in the foam, father!” they cried to Thorin.
“It is long enough without watering it.”
“Mind Bilbo doesn’t eat all the cakes!” they called.
“He is too fat to get through key-holes yet!”

“Hush, hush! Good People! and good night!” said Gandalf, who came last.
“Valleys have ears, and some elves have over merry tongues.
Good night!”

And so at last they all came to the Last Homely House, and found its doors flung wide.

They stayed long in that good house, fourteen days at least.
They found it hard to leave.
Bilbo would gladly have stopped there for ever and ever.
Even if a wish would have taken him right back to his hobbit-hole without trouble.

Yet there is little to tell about their stay.
The master of the house was Elrond.
He was an elf-friend.
He was as noble and as fair in face as an elf-lord.
He was as strong as a warrior.
He was as wise as a wizard.
He was as venerable as a king of dwarves.
And he was as kind as summer.

His house was perfect.
Whether you liked food, or sleep, or work, or story-telling, or singing, or just sitting and thinking best, or a pleasant mixture of them all, it was perfect.
Evil things did not come into that valley.

All of them, the ponies as well, grew refreshed and strong in a few days there.
Their clothes were mended as well as their bruises.
Their tempers and their hopes were mended too.
Their bags were filled with food and provisions light to carry but strong to bring them over the mountain passes.
Their plans were improved with the best advice.

So the time came to midsummer eve, and they were to go on again with the early sun on midsummer morning.

Elrond knew all about runes of every kind.
That day he looked at the swords they had brought from the trolls’ lair.
He said: “These are not troll-make.
They are old swords, very old swords of the High Elves of the West, my kin.
They were made in Gondolin for the Goblin-wars.
They must have come from a dragon’s hoard or goblin plunder.
For dragons and goblins destroyed that city many ages ago.

This, Thorin, the runes name Orcrist, the Goblin-cleaver in the ancient tongue of Gondolin.
It was a famous blade.
This, Gandalf, was Glamdring, Foe-hammer that the king of Gondolin once wore.
Keep them well!”

“Whence did the trolls get them, I wonder?” said Thorin looking at his sword with new interest.

“I could not say,” said Elrond, “but one may guess that your trolls had plundered other plunderers, or come on the remnants of old robberies in some hold in the mountains.”

Thorin pondered these words.
“I will keep this sword in honour,” he said.
“May it soon cleave goblins once again!”

“A wish that is likely to be granted soon enough in the mountains!” said Elrond.
“But show me now your map!”

He took it and gazed long at it.
He shook his head.
If he did not altogether approve of dwarves and their love of gold, he hated dragons and their cruel wickedness.
He grieved to remember the ruin of the town of Dale and its merry bells, and the burned banks of the bright River Running.

The moon was shining in a broad silver crescent.
He held up the map and the white light shone through it.

“What is this?” he said.
“There are moon-letters here, beside the plain runes which say ‘five feet high the door and three may walk abreast.’”

“What are moon-letters?” asked the hobbit full of excitement.
He loved maps.
He also liked runes and letters and cunning handwriting.

“Moon-letters are rune-letters, but you cannot see them,” said Elrond.
“Not when you look straight at them.
They can only be seen when the moon shines behind them.
And what is more, with the more cunning sort it must be a moon of the same shape and season as the day when they were written.
The dwarves invented them and wrote them with silver pens, as your friends could tell you.
These must have been written on a midsummer’s eve in a crescent moon, a long while ago.”

“What do they say?” asked Gandalf and Thorin together.

“Stand by the grey stone when the thrush knocks,” read Elrond, “and the setting sun with the last light of Durin’s Day will shine upon the key-hole.”

“Durin, Durin!” said Thorin.
“He was the father of the fathers of the eldest race of Dwarves, the Longbeards, and my first ancestor: I am his heir.”

“Then what is Durin’s Day?” asked Elrond.

“The first day of the dwarves’ New Year,” said Thorin, “is as all should know the first day of the last moon of Autumn on the threshold of Winter.
We still call it Durin’s Day when the last moon of Autumn and the sun are in the sky together.
But this will not help us much, I fear, for it passes our skill in these days to guess when such a time will come again.”

“That remains to be seen,” said Gandalf.

“Is there any more writing?”

“None to be seen by this moon,” said Elrond.
He gave the map back to Thorin.
Then they went down to the water to see the elves dance and sing upon the midsummer’s eve.

The next morning was a midsummer’s morning as fair and fresh as could be dreamed.
There was blue sky and never a cloud.
The sun was dancing on the water.
Now they rode away amid songs of farewell and good speed.
Their hearts were ready for more adventure.
They had a knowledge of the road they must follow over the Misty Mountains to the land beyond.
