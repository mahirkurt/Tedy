## Chapter II
## Roast Mutton

Up jumped Bilbo.
He put on his dressing-gown.
He went into the dining-room.

There he saw nobody.
But he saw all the signs of a large and hurried breakfast.
There was a fearful mess in the room.
There were piles of unwashed crocks in the kitchen.
Nearly every pot and pan he possessed seemed to have been used.

The washing-up was so dismally real.
Bilbo was forced to believe the party of the night before had not been part of his bad dreams.
He had rather hoped it was only a dream.

Indeed he was really relieved after all.
He thought they had all gone without him.
They had not bothered to wake him up.
“But with never a thank-you,” he thought.
And yet in a way he could not help feeling just a trifle disappointed.
The feeling surprised him.

“Don’t be a fool, Bilbo Baggins!” he said to himself.
“Thinking of dragons and all that outlandish nonsense at your age!”

So he put on an apron.
He lit fires.
He boiled water.
And he washed up.

Then he had a nice little breakfast in the kitchen.
After that he turned out the dining-room.

By that time the sun was shining.
The front door was open.
It was letting in a warm spring breeze.
Bilbo began to whistle loudly.
He began to forget about the night before.

In fact he was just sitting down to a nice little second breakfast.
He sat in the dining-room by the open window.
Then in walked Gandalf.

“My dear fellow,” said he, “whenever are you going to come?
What about an early start?
And here you are having breakfast, or whatever you call it, at half past ten!
They left you the message, because they could not wait.”

“What message?” said poor Mr. Baggins.
He was all in a fluster.

“Great Elephants!” said Gandalf.
“You are not at all yourself this morning.
You have never dusted the mantelpiece!”

“What’s that got to do with it?
I have had enough to do with washing up for fourteen!”

“If you had dusted the mantelpiece, you would have found this just under the clock,” said Gandalf.
He handed Bilbo a note.
It was written on Bilbo’s own note-paper.

This is what the note said:

“Thorin and Company to Burglar Bilbo greeting!
For your hospitality our sincerest thanks.
For your offer of professional assistance our grateful acceptance.
Terms: cash on delivery, up to and not exceeding one fourteenth of total profits (if any).
All travelling expenses guaranteed in any event.
Funeral expenses to be defrayed by us or our representatives, if occasion arises and the matter is not otherwise arranged for.

Thinking it unnecessary to disturb your esteemed repose, we have proceeded in advance to make requisite preparations.
We shall await your respected person at the Green Dragon Inn, Bywater, at 11 a.m. sharp.
Trusting that you will be punctual.

We have the honour to remain
Yours deeply
Thorin & Co.”

“That leaves you just ten minutes.
You will have to run,” said Gandalf.

“But—,” said Bilbo.

“No time for it,” said the wizard.

“But—,” said Bilbo again.

“No time for that either!
Off you go!”

To the end of his days Bilbo could never remember how he found himself outside.
He had no hat.
He had no walking-stick.
He had no money.
He had nothing that he usually took when he went out.
He left his second breakfast half-finished and quite unwashed-up.
He pushed his keys into Gandalf’s hands.
He ran as fast as his furry feet could carry him.
He ran down the lane.
He ran past the great Mill.
He ran across The Water.
Then he ran on for a mile or more.

Very puffed he was when he got to Bywater.
It was just on the stroke of eleven.
He found he had come without a pocket-handkerchief!

“Bravo!” said Balin.
Balin was standing at the inn door looking out for him.

Just then all the others came round the corner of the road from the village.
They were on ponies.
Each pony was slung about with all kinds of baggages, packages, parcels, and paraphernalia.
There was a very small pony, apparently for Bilbo.

“Up you two get, and off we go!” said Thorin.

“I’m awfully sorry,” said Bilbo.
“But I have come without my hat.
I have left my pocket-handkerchief behind.
I haven’t got any money.
I didn’t get your note until after 10.45 to be precise.”

“Don’t be precise,” said Dwalin.
“And don’t worry!
You will have to manage without pocket-handkerchiefs.
You will have to manage without a good many other things before you get to the journey’s end.
As for a hat, I have got a spare hood and cloak in my luggage.”

That’s how they all came to start.
They jogged off from the inn one fine morning just before May.
They were on laden ponies.
Bilbo was wearing a dark-green hood (a little weather-stained).
He was also wearing a dark-green cloak borrowed from Dwalin.
The clothes were too large for him.
He looked rather comic.
What his father Bungo would have thought of him, I daren’t think.
His only comfort was that he couldn’t be mistaken for a dwarf.
He had no beard.

They had not been riding very long when up came Gandalf.
He was very splendid on a white horse.
He had brought a lot of pocket-handkerchiefs.
He had also brought Bilbo’s pipe and tobacco.

So after that the party went along very merrily.
They told stories.
They sang songs as they rode forward all day.
Of course they stopped for meals.
These meals didn’t come quite as often as Bilbo would have liked them.
But still he began to feel that adventures were not so bad after all.

At first they had passed through hobbit-lands.
It was a wide respectable country.
It was inhabited by decent folk.
There were good roads.
There was an inn or two.
Now and then a dwarf or a farmer ambled by on business.

Then they came to lands where people spoke strangely.
They sang songs Bilbo had never heard before.

Now they had gone on far into the Lone-lands.
There were no people left.
There were no inns.
The roads grew steadily worse.
Not far ahead were dreary hills.
The hills rose higher and higher.
They were dark with trees.
On some of them were old castles with an evil look.
It looked as if they had been built by wicked people.

Everything seemed gloomy.
The weather that day had taken a nasty turn.
Mostly it had been as good as May can be.
But now it was cold and wet.

In the Lone-lands they had been obliged to camp when they could.
But at least it had been dry before.

“To think it will soon be June!” grumbled Bilbo.
He splashed along behind the others in a very muddy track.
It was after tea-time.
It was pouring with rain.
It had been raining all day.
His hood was dripping into his eyes.
His cloak was full of water.
The pony was tired and stumbled on stones.
The others were too grumpy to talk.

“And I’m sure the rain has got into the dry clothes and into the food-bags,” thought Bilbo.
“Bother burgling and everything to do with it!
I wish I was at home in my nice hole by the fire, with the kettle just beginning to sing!”

It was not the last time that he wished that.

Suddenly they saw a light.
The light was in the trees.
It was not far from the road.
They decided to go and look.

Bilbo went first.
He was the burglar.
He was supposed to go quietly.
He crept through the trees.
He came to a clearing.

There he saw three very large persons.
They were sitting round a big fire.
They were roasting mutton on long spits.
The mutton smelled very good.
There were two roasted sheep already.
There was a great jug of drink.

These were trolls.
Their names were William, Bert and Tom.
William was the biggest.
He was also the most talkative.
Bert was the one who did most of the cooking.
Tom was the one who complained a lot.

They were arguing.
They were talking about whether the mutton was done.
They were talking about how to cook the next batch.

Bilbo watched them for a long time.
He was frightened.
But he remembered he was the burglar.
He decided to try his luck.
He decided to pick one of their pockets.

He crept closer.
He crept very carefully.
He reached for William’s purse.
Just as his fingers touched it, William moved.
William felt something.

“What is it?” said William.
He looked round.
He saw Bilbo.

“A bobbit!” he cried.
“A bobbit has tried to pick my pocket!”

He grabbed Bilbo by the neck.
He lifted him up.
Bilbo kicked and struggled.
But he was much too small.

“What shall we do with it?” said William.
“Sit on it?” said Bert.
“No,” said Tom.
“Let’s roast it!”

They began to argue about the best way to cook a hobbit.
Some said roast.
Some said boil.
Some said sit on him.
Some said squash him.

While they argued, the dwarves came looking for Bilbo.
First Balin came.
The trolls caught him.
They put him in a sack.
Then the other dwarves came one by one.
Each time the trolls caught them.
Each time they put them in sacks.
Soon almost all the dwarves were in sacks.
The sacks were tied up.
The dwarves could not move.

Only Thorin was left free for a short time.
Then they caught him too.
They put him in a sack as well.

Now the trolls had thirteen dwarves in sacks.
They also had one hobbit.
They went back to arguing about how to cook them all.

William held Bilbo high in the air.
“What are you?” he asked.

“I am Bilbo Baggins,” said Bilbo.
His voice was shaking.
“I am a hobbit.”

“A hobbit?” said the trolls.
They looked at each other.
They had never seen a hobbit before.

“Can you cook them?” asked Tom.

“You can try,” said Bert.
He picked up a long skewer.

“He is too small,” said William.
“He would not make more than a mouthful.
Not even after you skin him and bone him.”

“Maybe there are more of them,” said Bert.
“We could make a pie.”
He looked at Bilbo’s furry feet.
“Hey you! Are there any more of your sort sneaking around these woods? You nasty little rabbit!”

He held Bilbo by the toes.
He shook him hard.

“Yes, lots,” said Bilbo at first.
Then he stopped himself.
He did not want to give his friends away.
“No. None at all. Not one,” he said quickly.

“What do you mean?” said Bert.
He turned Bilbo the right way up.
He held him by the hair.

“What I say,” said Bilbo.
He could hardly breathe.
“Please do not cook me, kind sirs!
I am a good cook myself.
I can cook better than I can cook, if you see what I mean.
I will cook a beautiful breakfast for you.
Only please do not have me for supper.”

“Poor little thing,” said William.
He had already eaten a lot.
He had also drunk a lot of beer.
“Let him go!”

“Not until he says what he means by ‘lots’ and ‘none at all’,” said Bert.
“I do not want my throat cut in my sleep.
Hold his toes in the fire until he talks!”

“I will not have it,” said William.
“I caught him.
He is mine.”

“You are a fat fool, William,” said Bert.

“And you are a lout!” said William.

Then they started to fight.
Bert hit William in the eye.
William hit Bert back.
Tom hit both of them with a branch.
They rolled on the ground.
They nearly rolled into the fire.
They shouted many true and nasty names at each other.

Bilbo fell to the ground.
His feet hurt.
He had no breath left.
He crawled a little way out of the firelight.
He lay there panting.

The trolls stopped fighting for a moment.
They looked at the sacks.
The sacks were full of dwarves.

“What are we going to do with them?” said Tom.

“Roast ’em!” said Bert.

“Boil ’em!” said Tom.

“Sit on ’em!” said William.
William always liked sitting on things.

“You’re always wanting to sit on things,” said Bert.
“I’ve told you before.
Sitting on them is no good.
They get all squashed.
Then there’s no meat left.”

“I like ’em squashed,” said William.

“Well I don’t,” said Bert.
“I say roast them.
Slow roast.
Over a low fire.
With lots of herbs.”

“Herbs?” said Tom.
“Where are we going to get herbs in these woods at night?”

“Then boil them,” said Tom.
“In a big pot.
With the mutton.
Make a big stew.”

“We haven’t got a pot big enough,” said William.
“Not for thirteen dwarves and a bobbit.”

“Then mince them,” said Bert.
“Mince them fine.
Make pies.”

“Mincing takes too long,” said Tom.
“I’m hungry now.”

They went on arguing.
They argued about roasting.
They argued about boiling.
They argued about mincing.
They argued about sitting on them.
They argued about the best sauce.
They argued about whether to cook them tonight or leave them for breakfast.

Every time one of them almost decided, another one disagreed.
The argument went on and on.

Suddenly a voice called from the trees.
It sounded exactly like William.

“Who’s a-arguing?” said the voice.
“I’m a-watching you.
You’re a-wasting time.”

William looked round.
“I never said that!” he shouted.

Then another voice came.
It sounded like Bert.
“You’re a fool, William,” said the voice.

“I never said that either!” yelled Bert.

The voices kept coming from different places in the dark trees.
The trolls became more and more angry with each other.
They forgot about the dwarves for a while.
They started fighting again.

Gandalf was the one making the voices.
He was hidden in the trees.
He kept them arguing and fighting until the sky began to grow light.

When the first light of dawn came, the trolls were still quarrelling.
The light grew stronger.
Suddenly William looked at his hands.
They were turning to stone.
Bert tried to move.
He could not.
Tom opened his mouth to shout.
No sound came.

The three trolls turned to stone.
They stood there like three big ugly statues.
The sun had caught them.

Gandalf came out of the trees.
He laughed.
“Well, well!” he said.
“That was a near thing.
I arrived just in time.”

Then the company freed the dwarves from the sacks.
They were very glad to be free.
They were also very glad the trolls were gone.

They searched the trolls’ cave.
Inside they found many things.
They found gold.
They found silver.
They found weapons.
They found food.

Thorin found a beautiful sword.
It was an old sword.
It had been made by the elves long ago.
Its name was Orcrist.
Gandalf found another fine sword.
Its name was Glamdring.
Bilbo found a small knife.
It was just the right size for a hobbit.
Later he would call it Sting.

They took what they needed.
They buried some of the gold for later.
Then they continued their journey.
They went towards the Lonely Mountain.

Bilbo walked beside his pony for a while.
He still felt shaken by the night’s adventure.
But he also felt a small pride.
He had tried to be a burglar, even if he had been caught.
The real adventure had begun.
