## Chapter I
## An Unexpected Party

In a hole in the ground there lived a hobbit.

It was not a nasty hole. It was not a dirty hole. It was not a wet hole. It was not filled with the ends of worms. It did not have an oozy smell.

It was not a dry hole either. It was not a bare hole. It was not a sandy hole. There was plenty to sit on. There was plenty to eat.

It was a hobbit-hole. And that means comfort.

The door was perfectly round. It looked like a porthole. The door was painted green. There was a shiny yellow brass knob. The knob was right in the exact middle of the door.

The door opened onto a tube-shaped hall. The hall was like a tunnel. It was a very comfortable tunnel. There was no smoke in it. The walls were panelled. The floors were tiled. The floors were also carpeted. There were polished chairs. There were lots and lots of pegs. The pegs were for hats and coats. The hobbit was fond of visitors.

The tunnel wound on and on. It went into the side of the hill. It went fairly straight, but not quite straight. The people for many miles around called this place The Hill.

Many little round doors opened out of the tunnel. Some doors were on one side. Some doors were on the other side.

There was no going upstairs for the hobbit. Everything was on the same floor. There were bedrooms. There were bathrooms. There were cellars. There were pantries. There were lots of pantries. There were wardrobes. He had whole rooms just for clothes. There were kitchens. There were dining-rooms. All of them were on the same passage.

The best rooms were all on the left-hand side when you went in. These rooms had windows. The windows were deep-set. The windows were round. They looked over his garden. They looked over the meadows beyond. The meadows sloped down to the river.

This hobbit was a very well-to-do hobbit. His name was Baggins.

The Bagginses had lived near The Hill for a very long time. People considered them very respectable. Most of them were rich. They never had any adventures. They never did anything unexpected. You could tell what a Baggins would say on any question. You did not even need to ask him.

This is a story of how a Baggins had an adventure. He found himself doing unexpected things. He found himself saying unexpected things. He may have lost the neighbours’ respect. But he gained something else. You will see whether he gained anything in the end.

What is a hobbit? I suppose hobbits need some description. They have become rare now. They are shy of the Big People. That is what they call us.

They are a little people. They are about half our height. They are smaller than the bearded Dwarves. Hobbits have no beards.

There is little or no magic about them. There is only the ordinary everyday sort of magic. It helps them to disappear quietly and quickly. This happens when large stupid folk like you and me come along. We make a noise like elephants. They can hear us a mile off.

They are inclined to be fat in the stomach. They dress in bright colours. They like green and yellow most of all. They wear no shoes. Their feet grow natural leathery soles. Their feet have thick warm brown hair. The hair is like the stuff on their heads. Their hair is curly.

They have long clever brown fingers. They have good-natured faces. They laugh deep fruity laughs. They especially laugh after dinner. They have dinner twice a day when they can get it.

Now you know enough to go on with.

As I was saying, the mother of this hobbit was the famous Belladonna Took. This hobbit was Bilbo Baggins. Belladonna was one of the three remarkable daughters of the Old Took. The Old Took was the head of the hobbits who lived across The Water. The Water was the small river that ran at the foot of The Hill.

It was often said in other families that long ago one of the Took ancestors must have taken a fairy wife. That was, of course, absurd. But there was still something not entirely hobbit-like about them. Once in a while members of the Took-clan would go and have adventures. They discreetly disappeared. The family hushed it up. But the fact remained. The Tooks were not as respectable as the Bagginses. They were undoubtedly richer, though.

Not that Belladonna Took ever had any adventures after she became Mrs. Bungo Baggins. Bungo was Bilbo’s father. He built the most luxurious hobbit-hole for her. He built it partly with her money. It was the best hobbit-hole under The Hill or over The Hill or across The Water. They remained there to the end of their days.

Still, it is probable that Bilbo, her only son, got something a bit queer in his make-up from the Took side. He looked and behaved exactly like a second edition of his solid and comfortable father. But something waited for a chance to come out.

The chance never arrived until Bilbo Baggins was grown up. He was about fifty years old or so. He was living in the beautiful hobbit-hole built by his father.

One morning long ago, in the quiet of the world, there was less noise and more green. The hobbits were still numerous and prosperous. Bilbo Baggins was standing at his door after breakfast. He was smoking an enormous long wooden pipe. The pipe reached nearly down to his woolly toes. His toes were neatly brushed.

Gandalf came by.

He was an old man. He had a staff. He had a tall pointed blue hat. He had a long grey cloak. He had a silver scarf. His long white beard hung down below his waist. He wore immense black boots.

“Good Morning!” said Bilbo. He meant it. The sun was shining. The grass was very green.

But Gandalf looked at him. He looked from under long bushy eyebrows. The eyebrows stuck out further than the brim of his shady hat.

“What do you mean?” he said. “Do you wish me a good morning? Or do you mean that it is a good morning whether I want it or not? Or that you feel good this morning? Or that it is a morning to be good on?”

“All of them at once,” said Bilbo. “And a very fine morning for a pipe of tobacco out of doors. If you have a pipe about you, sit down. Have a fill of mine! There’s no hurry. We have all the day before us!”

Then Bilbo sat down on a seat by his door. He crossed his legs. He blew out a beautiful grey ring of smoke. The ring sailed up into the air without breaking. It floated away over The Hill.

“Very pretty!” said Gandalf. “But I have no time to blow smoke-rings this morning. I am looking for someone to share in an adventure that I am arranging. It is very difficult to find anyone.”

“I should think so—in these parts!” said Bilbo. “We are plain quiet folk. We have no use for adventures. Adventures are nasty things. They are disturbing things. They are uncomfortable things. They make you late for dinner! I can’t think what anybody sees in them.”

He stuck one thumb behind his braces. He blew out another even bigger smoke-ring. Then he took out his morning letters. He began to read. He pretended to take no more notice of the old man. He had decided that the old man was not quite his sort. He wanted him to go away.

But the old man did not move. He stood leaning on his stick. He gazed at the hobbit. He said nothing. Bilbo got quite uncomfortable. He even got a little cross.

“Good morning!” he said at last. “We don’t want any adventures here, thank you! You might try over The Hill or across The Water.” By this he meant that the conversation was at an end.

“What a lot of things you do use Good morning for!” said Gandalf. “Now you mean that you want to get rid of me, and that it won’t be good till I move off.”

“Not at all, not at all, my dear sir! Let me see, I don’t think I know your name?”

“Yes, yes, my dear sir—and I do know your name, Mr. Bilbo Baggins. And you do know my name, though you don’t remember that I belong to it. I am Gandalf, and Gandalf means me! To think that I should have lived to be good-morninged by Belladonna Took’s son, as if I was selling buttons at the door!”

“Gandalf, Gandalf! Good gracious me! Not the wandering wizard that gave Old Took a pair of magic diamond studs that fastened themselves and never came undone till ordered? Not the fellow who used to tell such wonderful tales at parties, about dragons and goblins and giants and the rescue of princesses and the unexpected luck of widows’ sons? Not the man that used to make such particularly excellent fireworks! I remember those! Old Took used to have them on Midsummer’s Eve. Splendid! They used to go up like great lilies and snapdragons and laburnums of fire and hang in the twilight all evening!”

You will notice already that Mr. Baggins was not quite so prosaic as he liked to believe. You will also notice that he was very fond of flowers.

“Dear me!” he went on. “Not the Gandalf who was responsible for so many quiet lads and lasses going off into the Blue for mad adventures? Anything from climbing trees to visiting elves—or sailing in ships, sailing to other shores! Bless me, life used to be quite inter—I mean, you used to upset things badly in these parts once upon a time. I beg your pardon, but I had no idea you were still in business.”

“Where else should I be?” said the wizard. “All the same I am pleased to find you remember something about me. You seem to remember my fireworks kindly, at any rate, and that is not without hope. Indeed for your old grandfather Took’s sake, and for the sake of poor Belladonna, I will give you what you asked for.”

“I beg your pardon, I haven’t asked for anything!”

“Yes, you have! Twice now. My pardon. I give it you. In fact I will go so far as to send you on this adventure. Very amusing for me, very good for you—and profitable too, very likely, if you ever get over it.”

“Sorry! I don’t want any adventures, thank you. Not today. Good morning! But please come to tea—any time you like! Why not tomorrow? Come tomorrow! Good bye!”

With that the hobbit turned and scuttled inside his round green door. He shut it as quickly as he dared, not to seem rude. Wizards after all are wizards.

“What on earth did I ask him to tea for!” he said to himself, as he went to the pantry. He had only just had breakfast. But he thought a cake or two and a drink of something would do him good after his fright.

Gandalf in the meantime was still standing outside the door. He was laughing long but quietly. After a while he stepped up. With the spike on his staff he scratched a queer sign on the hobbit’s beautiful green front-door. Then he strode away. He left just about the time when Bilbo was finishing his second cake. Bilbo was beginning to think that he had escaped adventures very well.

The next day he had almost forgotten about Gandalf. He did not remember things very well, unless he put them down on his Engagement Tablet. He would write something like this: Gandalf Tea Wednesday. Yesterday he had been too flustered to do anything of the kind.

Just before tea-time there came a tremendous ring on the front-door bell. Then he remembered! He rushed and put on the kettle. He put out another cup and saucer. He put out an extra cake or two. He ran to the door.

“I am so sorry to keep you waiting!” he was going to say. Then he saw that it was not Gandalf at all. It was a dwarf with a blue beard tucked into a golden belt. He had very bright eyes under his dark-green hood. As soon as the door was opened, he pushed inside, just as if he had been expected.

He hung his hooded cloak on the nearest peg. “Dwalin at your service!” he said with a low bow.

“Bilbo Baggins at yours!” said the hobbit. He was too surprised to ask any questions for the moment. When the silence that followed had become uncomfortable, he added: “I am just about to take tea; pray come and have some with me.” It was a little stiff perhaps, but he meant it kindly. And what would you do, if an uninvited dwarf came and hung his things up in your hall without a word of explanation?

They had not been at table long. In fact they had hardly reached the third cake. Then there came another even louder ring at the bell.

“Excuse me!” said the hobbit, and off he went to the door.

“So you have got here at last!” That was what he was going to say to Gandalf this time. But it was not Gandalf. Instead there was a very old-looking dwarf on the step. He had a white beard and a scarlet hood. He too hopped inside as soon as the door was open, just as if he had been invited.

“I see they have begun to arrive already,” he said when he caught sight of Dwalin’s green hood hanging up. He hung his red one next to it. “Balin at your service!” he said with his hand on his breast.

“Thank you!” said Bilbo with a gasp. It was not the correct thing to say. The words “they have begun to arrive” had flustered him badly. He liked visitors. But he liked to know them before they arrived. He preferred to ask them himself. He had a horrible thought that the cakes might run short. Then he, as the host, might have to go without. He knew his duty and stuck to it, however painful.

“Come along in, and have some tea!” he managed to say after taking a deep breath.

“A little beer would suit me better, if it is all the same to you, my good sir,” said Balin with the white beard. “But I don’t mind some cake—seed-cake, if you have any.”

“Lots!” Bilbo found himself answering, to his own surprise. He found himself scuttling off to the cellar to fill a pint beer-mug. Then he went to a pantry to fetch two beautiful round seed-cakes. He had baked them that afternoon for his after-supper morsel.

When he got back, Balin and Dwalin were talking at the table like old friends. As a matter of fact they were brothers. Bilbo plumped down the beer and the cake in front of them. Then a loud ring came at the bell again, and then another ring.

“Gandalf for certain this time,” he thought as he puffed along the passage. But it was not. It was two more dwarves. Both had blue hoods, silver belts, and yellow beards. Each of them carried a bag of tools and a spade. In they hopped, as soon as the door began to open. Bilbo was hardly surprised at all.

“What can I do for you, my dwarves?” he said.

“Kili at your service!” said the one. “And Fili!” added the other. They both swept off their blue hoods and bowed.

“At yours and your family’s!” replied Bilbo, remembering his manners this time.

“Dwalin and Balin here already, I see,” said Kili. “Let us join the throng!”

“Throng!” thought Mr. Baggins. “I don’t like the sound of that. I really must sit down for a minute and collect my wits, and have a drink.” He had only just had a sip in the corner. The four dwarves sat round the table. They talked about mines and gold and troubles with the goblins. They talked about the damage done by dragons, and lots of other things which he did not understand. He did not want to understand them. They sounded much too adventurous. Then, ding-dong-a-ling-dang, his bell rang again. It was as if some naughty little hobbit-boy was trying to pull the handle off.

“Someone at the door!” he said, blinking.

“Some four, I should say by the sound,” said Fili. “Besides, we saw them looking in through the window.”

The next four dwarves who came in were very different. Two of them had yellow hair and yellow beards. That is unusual for dwarves. They wore dark-green hoods and bright yellow stockings. The other two had dark hair and dark beards and wore dark-blue hoods. They all had heavy packs and tools of different kinds. When they had all introduced themselves and hung up their hoods, Bilbo brought more food and more drink. The kitchen was beginning to look like a large party. Then the door-bell rang yet again.

This time more dwarves arrived. In came Dori, Nori and Ori. Then Oin and Gloin. Then Bifur, Bofur and Bombur. Bombur was especially fat and needed a strong chair. The hall was now crowded with hoods and cloaks hanging on every peg. Bags and tools lay all over the floor. Bilbo’s pantry and cellar were almost empty. He was beginning to feel that his house had been taken over. At last the door-bell rang once more, louder than before.

“Now we are all here!” said a deep voice. “I’ve brought the key.” It was Gandalf. Behind him stood Thorin Oakenshield.

Thorin was an important dwarf and looked it. He had a long blue beard tucked into a golden belt. He had very bright eyes under his dark-green hood. He wore a large gold chain. He spoke in a deep, serious voice. He was the leader of the company, and the most important person present after Gandalf.

There were thirteen dwarves in all. Their names were Dwalin, Balin, Kili, Fili, Dori, Nori, Ori, Oin, Gloin, Bifur, Bofur, Bombur, and Thorin.

They all sat down to a large meal. Bilbo was kept very busy. He ran backwards and forwards between the kitchen, the pantry and the cellar. He carried plates, cups, dishes, more seed-cakes, more bread, more cheese, more cold meats and pickles, and more beer. The dwarves ate and drank a great deal. They were very merry. They talked and laughed in loud voices. They told stories of the old days under the Lonely Mountain. They spoke of gold and jewels and the great halls that had once been theirs. They sang songs in their deep voices.

One of the songs was long and sad and full of anger. It told of the dwarves’ lost home under the Lonely Mountain. It told of the day the dragon Smaug had come. The dragon had burned the towns of men nearby. He had driven the dwarves out. He had killed many of them. He had taken all the treasure and guarded it ever since. The song spoke of the need to return and take back what was theirs. The music filled the whole room. Bilbo felt both excited and afraid. Something inside him, the Tookish part, began to wake up. He wanted to see mountains and deep caves. He wanted to see the dragon. At the same time his knees felt weak and he wished he was back in his quiet hole.

The dwarves sang more songs. Some were cheerful. Some were fierce. They stamped their feet and beat on the table. The noise was tremendous. Bilbo’s little dining-room had never heard anything like it. He felt smaller and smaller as the evening went on. He kept bringing more food and more drink until there was almost nothing left in the house.

After the singing, Gandalf stood up. He took from his cloak a large map and a small key. He spread the map carefully on the table so that everyone could see it. The map showed the lands far away to the east. It showed the Lonely Mountain standing by itself. It showed the river that ran down from the mountain and the forests around it. Most important of all, it marked a secret door on the side of the mountain. The little key was the key that would open that door.

Thorin then spoke for a long time. He told the full story of how the dwarves had once lived under the Lonely Mountain in great wealth and happiness. Their halls had been filled with gold and jewels and beautiful things made by their own skilled hands. Then the dragon Smaug had come from the North. He had destroyed the nearby towns of the men of the lake. He had driven the dwarves from their home. He had killed many, including members of Thorin’s own family. He had taken every piece of treasure and made a bed of gold for himself deep under the mountain. Thorin’s grandfather Thror and his father Thrain had escaped with their lives, but they had lost their kingdom and their wealth. Now Thorin wished to return with a small company of trusted dwarves. He wished to enter by the secret door shown on the map. He wished to reclaim the treasure that belonged by right to his people. But for this they needed a burglar—someone small, quiet and clever who could slip inside first, learn about the dragon, and open the way. Gandalf had chosen Bilbo Baggins for this dangerous work.

Bilbo was horrified. He stood up from his chair and said in a clear voice that he was not a burglar. He said he was a respectable hobbit. He had never stolen so much as a spoon in his life. He did not want any adventures of any kind. He wanted only to stay in his own comfortable hole with his own things. Some of the dwarves laughed at him. Others looked disappointed and muttered. Thorin looked angry and proud, as if he thought Bilbo was not worth the trouble. But Gandalf spoke firmly. He said that Bilbo possessed both the careful, quiet habits of a Baggins and the more adventurous spirit that sometimes appeared in the Took family. He said that Bilbo was exactly the person they needed. After a long argument, with much talking back and forth, Bilbo at last agreed. He signed a paper that Gandalf had prepared. The paper was a proper contract. It stated that Bilbo Baggins would receive one fourteenth share of all the profits of the adventure, if any profits were made. It also stated that he would bear his full share of the dangers and the risks of the journey.

The party continued late into the night. The dwarves told more stories. They spoke of the road that lay ahead, of the wild lands, of the Misty Mountains, and of the dangers they might meet. Bilbo listened with wide eyes. He began to feel that he had got himself into something far bigger than a simple tea-party. He felt both proud and terrified.

When the eating and the singing finally ended, the dwarves cleaned the kitchen and the dining-room with surprising speed and tidiness. They washed the plates and put everything back in its place. Then they chose bedrooms in different parts of the hobbit-hole and went to sleep. Some slept in the best rooms. Others made do with smaller ones. Bombur needed a very strong bed.

Bilbo lay in his own bed in the dark. He could hear Thorin still humming the song about the misty mountains in the best bedroom next door:

> Far over the misty mountains cold
> To dungeons deep and caverns old
> We must away, ere break of day,
> To find our long-forgotten gold.

Bilbo went to sleep with that song in his ears. It gave him very uncomfortable dreams. He dreamed of dragons and dark mountains and long roads. It was long after the break of day when he woke up.
